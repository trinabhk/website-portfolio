SEO in Nepal: What Actually Works for Local Businesses

Most of my hands-on SEO work happens on sites built and run from Nepal - small business sites, e-commerce stores, and CMS-based projects on hosts like GoDaddy, Bluehost, and cPanel. The fundamentals of SEO don't change by country, but where Nepali businesses lose rankings tends to follow a pattern worth writing down.

How people search here

Google is overwhelmingly the dominant search engine in Nepal, and most of that traffic is mobile. Search behavior is mixed-language in practice - a query might be in English, transliterated Nepali, or a mix of both in the same session - and local intent ("near me," a city name, a specific neighborhood) shows up constantly for service businesses. A site that isn't mobile-friendly, or that only targets stiff English keyword phrases nobody actually types, is already leaving visibility on the table before any technical issue even enters the picture.

Where local sites lose rankings

Across the sites I've audited, the same handful of issues show up again and again:

  • No verified Search Console property. A large share of small business sites in Nepal have never been verified in Google Search Console at all, which means nobody is watching for indexing errors, security issues, or manual actions until traffic has already dropped.
  • Leftover staging settings. Sites built by an agency or freelancer and launched without a final check often ship with a stray noindex tag or a Disallow: / left in robots.txt from the development phase - invisible until someone checks the source.
  • Broken migrations. Moving hosts (a common cost-saving move) without mapping old URLs to new ones creates redirect chains or straight 404s, and any ranking history on those URLs evaporates.
  • No Google Business Profile, or an unclaimed/inconsistent one. For any business with a physical location or service area, this is often worth more than on-page tweaks - it's what shows up in the local map pack, which is where a lot of "near me" searches actually get answered.
  • Thin or duplicate category pages on small e-commerce stores, which Google treats as Soft 404s and drops from the index without much warning.
// the pattern Almost none of these are content problems. They're configuration problems - the kind that don't show up by looking at the site in a browser, only by checking what Google actually sees.

A starting checklist

If you run a business site in Nepal and aren't sure where you stand, this is roughly the order I'd check things in:

  1. Verify the property in Google Search Console (and Bing Webmaster Tools) if it isn't already.
  2. Check robots.txt and every key page's meta robots tag for accidental blocks left over from development.
  3. Claim and complete a Google Business Profile if the business serves customers locally.
  4. Confirm the site works cleanly on mobile - this is non-negotiable given how much search traffic here is mobile.
  5. Submit a real XML sitemap and check the Coverage report for errors.
  6. If the site has migrated hosts or domains recently, verify every important old URL 301s in a single hop.

This is close to the same sequence I lay out in more technical detail in my audit checklist - the difference here is just which issues show up most often on the sites I see locally.

Why this gets overlooked

A lot of Nepali business sites are built fast and cheap, by a developer whose job ends at "the site is live." That's a reasonable scope for a first build, but it usually means nobody ever circles back to check what Google is seeing. The gap between "the site looks fine" and "the site is indexable, crawlable, and actually visible" is exactly where I spend most of my time.

The businesses that win locally aren't necessarily the ones with the biggest budget - they're the ones whose sites Google can actually read.
